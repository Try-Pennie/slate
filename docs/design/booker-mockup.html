Slate booker

The screen a lead sees when they book a call from a Pennie landing page. It opens in a modal on the page they are already on, so the whole thing is one step wide and never navigates them away.

The flow

Four steps, all in the same panel. Click through it: pick a day, pick a time, add details, done.

The day strip shows the next four days a lead can actually book. Closed days are not drawn at all, so there is no greyed out remainder to explain, and the dashed rule marks where days were skipped. The month appears under the date wherever it changes, so the turn of a month never reads as the past.

Every other state

What the panel shows when it is not the happy path.

Loading
Pick a day

Finding the days you can book.

Placeholder chips rather than a blank gap, so the area reads as deliberately loading. No time skeletons sit below them: times belong to a chosen day, and no day has been chosen yet, so drawing rows there would promise a list that will not arrive on its own. The pulse stops for anyone who has asked for reduced motion.

Could not load
Pick a day

Could not load available times. Check your connection.

A failure and an empty diary are different things and must never look alike. This one says the connection failed and offers a retry, rather than implying nobody is free.

Nothing free in that day
Pick a day

The message names the day that came up empty, so nobody has to work out what another one would be. That day stays selected and the rest stay in reach, so a lead is one tap from a time rather than back at the start.

Closed for a while
Pick a day

When the office is shut long enough that nothing is bookable nearby, the panel says so and names the day we are back. The button then shows the full set of days, not one. It only appears for a genuinely long closure; an ordinary holiday weekend just shows the days.

Already have a call booked

We only learn who someone is when they hand over their details, so a call they already have cannot be spotted while they are picking a time. It surfaces the moment they confirm, and the panel puts that call in front of them to move or to keep.

The call they already have is the subject here, not a rule they have run into. It is named with the day, the time and the advisor, so they can recognise which one it is, and moving it to the time they just picked is one tap. Keeping it is right there too, for anyone who opened the booker by accident. Moving leaves one call on one calendar rather than two an advisor has to reconcile, and nothing is cancelled on their behalf either way.

Changing or cancelling

The link in the confirmation, and in the calendar invite, opens this. It is a page of its own rather than the modal, since a lead arrives at it cold, often days later.

Rescheduling runs the booking flow again rather than a second design of it: the same day strip, the same wait for a day before any time appears. Choosing it also clears the page down to the reason and the new time, so Cancel booking is not sitting beside the times a lead is reading, and one quiet link goes back. Cancelling asks once before it happens, and the confirm is worded so the two buttons cannot be misread in a hurry. A cancelled booking does not dead end: it offers a way back to the same team rather than leaving someone stuck on a page that says no.

On a phone, and inside the embed

The modal is 92% of the viewport at most, so on a narrow screen the panels stack and the strip stays four across.

The event details sit above rather than beside, times drop to a single column, and every target stays at least 44 pixels so it is comfortable with a thumb. It waits for a day the same way the full width panel does, because it is the same picker and not a phone shaped copy of one.
